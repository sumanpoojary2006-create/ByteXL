## Introduction

Scrum's events are short, but they carry the framework. Planning gives the sprint direction, the daily check-in keeps the team aligned, the review gathers feedback, and the retrospective improves the team's own method. When these events are done well, they replace a great deal of status reporting. When they are done badly, they become meetings that waste time.

**Definition:** The Scrum events are time-boxed meetings that give each sprint a purpose, keep the team coordinated, collect stakeholder feedback, and improve the way of working: sprint planning decides what to do, the daily stand-up synchronises the team, and the retrospective turns experience into improvement.

## Sprint Planning

Planning answers three questions.

1. **Why is this sprint valuable?** The Product Owner proposes how the product could increase in value, and the team agrees a **Sprint Goal**, a single purpose that gives coherence to the work.
2. **What can be done?** The Developers select backlog items that they believe can be completed, using their experience of earlier sprints and their capacity (for example, planned absences).
3. **How will the chosen work get done?** The team breaks the selected items into small tasks and agrees an initial plan.

Good planning depends on a backlog that has already been prepared. The better the team understands an item before the meeting, the shorter planning takes.

## The Daily Scrum (Stand-up)

The daily stand-up is a 15-minute event for the Developers, held at the same time and place each day. Its purpose is **to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan for the next 24 hours**, not to report status to a manager.

A common structure, though the Scrum Guide leaves the format to the team, is for each person to cover three points: what I did that helps toward the goal, what I will do next, and what is blocking me. A different effective approach walks the board from the right, asking what is needed to move each item towards Done.

Signs that a stand-up is working: it is brief, focused on the goal, produces concrete follow-ups, and ends with a clear shared picture of the day. Signs that it is not working: it is a round of individual reports to one person, it overruns, or problems are mentioned but never resolved.

## Sprint Review

The review is a working session, not a presentation. The team shows what has been completed, stakeholders respond, and together they discuss what to do next. The outcome is an updated product backlog. It is held at the end of the sprint, before the retrospective.

## The Retrospective

The retrospective is where the team improves itself. It looks at people, relationships, process, and tools, asks what went well, what caused problems, and what to change, and agrees **at least one concrete improvement** to try in the next sprint.

A retrospective should be **blameless**: it examines how the work flowed, not who is at fault. A common weakness is a list of complaints with no action. A strong practice is to start the next retrospective by reviewing whether last time's actions were completed.

## Backlog Refinement

Between events, teams spend time **refining** the product backlog: clarifying, splitting, and estimating upcoming items. It is not a formal event in the Scrum Guide, but it is what keeps planning short. A common guideline is to spend a small share of each sprint on refinement so that the next sprint's items are well understood.

## Your Turn: Which Event Fits?

1. The team decides to add a short written checklist before work is declared finished, after noticing repeated mistakes.
2. A developer says they cannot proceed because they are waiting on another team, and a teammate offers to help.
3. The team and the Product Owner agree the single purpose of the next two weeks.

Item 1 belongs to the retrospective: a concrete improvement in how the team works. Item 2 belongs to the daily stand-up: a blocker is surfaced and a plan to resolve it is made. Item 3 belongs to sprint planning: agreeing the Sprint Goal.

## Conclusion

The Scrum events are time-boxed meetings that give each sprint a purpose, keep the team coordinated, collect stakeholder feedback, and improve the way of working: sprint planning decides what to do, the daily stand-up synchronises the team, and the retrospective turns experience into improvement. Their value comes from focus and follow-through.

Scrum organises the management of the work. The next topic describes a method that concentrates on the technical practices of building software well.
