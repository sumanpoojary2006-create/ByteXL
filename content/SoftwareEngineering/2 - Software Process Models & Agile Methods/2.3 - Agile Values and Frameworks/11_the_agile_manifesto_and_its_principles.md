## Introduction

The Agile Manifesto is a short statement of values and principles for developing software, written in 2001 by a group of practitioners who had each used lighter alternatives to heavy, document-driven processes. It states what the signatories valued more and what they valued less, and it is supported by twelve principles.

This topic presents that statement, the Agile Manifesto, and the principles behind it, and corrects the most common misunderstandings.

**Definition:** Agile is a family of approaches to software development, based on the values and principles of the Agile Manifesto, that favour individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, and responding to change, delivered through short iterations and continuous feedback.

The manifesto adds that **while there is value in the items on the right, the items on the left are valued more.** It does not reject processes, documentation, contracts, or planning. It sets priorities when they conflict.

## What Each Value Means

- **Individuals and interactions.** Good software comes from capable people communicating well. The best process cannot rescue a team that does not talk.
- **Working software.** Progress is measured by software that works, not by documents or promises. A running system is the most honest status report.
- **Customer collaboration.** Customers are partners throughout, not adversaries to be managed by a contract. Their needs are discovered through continuing conversation.
- **Responding to change.** Change is normal, and the ability to adapt is more valuable than the ability to follow an original plan faithfully.

## Agile Is Not a Single Method

Agile is an umbrella, not a recipe. Scrum, Extreme Programming, Kanban, and Lean are specific frameworks and practices that apply agile values in different ways. A team can be agile without using any one of them by name, and a team can use their vocabulary without being agile in spirit.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"Agile means no documentation."** It means documentation that serves a purpose, not documentation as a goal.
- **"Agile means no planning."** Planning is continuous and in smaller steps.
- **"Agile means going fast and cutting corners."** The principles insist on technical excellence and a sustainable pace.
- **"Agile means the customer can demand anything at any time."** Change is welcomed, but priorities are explicit and capacity is finite.
- **"Agile is a set of ceremonies."** Holding meetings with agile names does not make a team agile; the values matter.

## Your Turn: Which Value Is at Stake?

1. A team argues over whether to keep a very long specification up to date or to talk to users and ship a small working feature.
2. A customer asks for a change late in the project; the team discusses whether to adapt or to insist on the original plan.
3. A manager wants to judge progress by the number of documents completed.

Situation 1 involves "working software over comprehensive documentation" and customer collaboration. Situation 2 involves "responding to change over following a plan." Situation 3 conflicts with the principle that working software is the primary measure of progress.

## Conclusion

Agile is a family of approaches to software development, based on the values and principles of the Agile Manifesto, that favour individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, and responding to change, delivered through short iterations and continuous feedback. It is a mindset first and a set of practices second.

The most widely used framework built on these values is Scrum. The next topic describes its roles, events, and artifacts.
